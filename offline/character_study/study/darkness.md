# Darkness

The first time Kazuma sees her, he sees a body. A very curvy body, a cold beauty in full plate mail, someone whose face, he thinks, picks at your masochistic side. It is the first of many times the narration will notice her body before it notices anything else about her. She will spend the entire story being seen this way—by Kazuma, by her comrades, by suitors, by enemies, by the reader—and she will spend just as long insisting that this is both exactly what she wants and not at all who she is.

The trick of Darkness is that both claims are true at once, and neither one explains her fully.

Her introduction contains the whole contradiction. A cool-looking knight named by her friend as "this person with a moody face" grabs Kazuma's hand, tightens her grip when he mentions slime-covered girls, and blurts out that she wants to experience being ravaged by cabbages and monsters before catching herself and pretending to walk it back. She announces that she cannot hit anything with her sword but is very good at being a shield. She begs to be abused, then says being hit feels better without armor, then denies saying it. By the end of her first proper scene, the narrator has filed her under "super masochist," and that file will follow her for hundreds of thousands of words. She looks like a cool beauty, Kazuma concludes, but she is just a pervert.

That is the joke, and the joke is not wrong. Darkness is a super masochist. She fantasizes about being kept as a degraded pet, gets aroused by public humiliation, offers herself as bait, as hostage, as sacrifice, as anything that will let her be used and mistreated and survived. She converts a forced prostration in the snow into "a reward." She cannot hear about slimes without imagining what it would be like to be dissolved by one. When the party visits a city full of religious fanatics who despise her goddess, she is spat at, stoned, served dog food, and swept with trash, and her trembling refusal to hide her amulet is inseparable from her enjoyment of the persecution. In a bath she believes she shares with a princess, she strips without hesitation and offers to be washed. In a dojo she kneels, flushed and panting, demanding to know what punishment Kazuma has planned for her. The comedy is relentless. The comedy is also, sometimes, a mask worn so tightly it has become indistinguishable from face.

Because underneath the masochism—or rather, fused into it—is a knight. She will not bow to a monster out of fear, even if no one is watching. She will charge an enemy without armor because someone has to. She will plant herself before a falling fortress and refuse to retreat one step because she has an obligation to protect the citizens of this town, an obligation she cannot yet explain. When Beldia aims a death curse at Megumin, Darkness grabs Megumin and hides her behind her own body, taking the curse herself. When the Winter Shogun breaks her sword and Kazuma asks if she is worried, she answers that she is confident in her toughness and her weapon would not hit anyway—a line of such flat, unself-pitying self-knowledge that it sounds less like a joke and more like a confession.

Her prayer over the dead Beldia is the first sign that something in her is not comic at all. She kneels and prays for the knight who was unjustly executed and became a monster, because there is no reason not to. She mourns the adventurers he killed, remembers their names and their flaws and says she would want to drink with them again. She says this with such gentleness that it is hard to recognize her as the woman who, minutes earlier, was volunteering to be a shield for the sake of being hit. And then, when she realizes that someone has heard this private prayer, she covers her red face and says, "I want to die." This, she insists, is not the kind of humiliation she wants. The distinction she draws—between public degradation and private sincerity—will matter throughout her life.

The Keele's Dungeon episode gives that private self a voice and then takes it away again. Listening to the story of a lich who eloped with a nobleman's daughter and spent years on the run with her, Darkness says, with complete conviction and a lonely smile, that the lady's fugitive life was one of the happiest times of her life. The narrator notes that her words seemed to have hidden meanings, and Darkness does not explain them. Something of her own life seems to pass through the remark: she has been a noble's daughter all along, and the thought of escape is not foreign to her.

When the trial reveals what she has been hiding—that she is Dustiness ford Lalatina, daughter of the national chief adviser, scion of a house known as the Beloved Shield of the Kingdom—it reframes everything that came before. Her taste for degradation can no longer be considered apart from a life of high expectations, formal manners, and political bargaining, though that life does not explain the taste away. Her armor is not the hobby of an enthusiast but the uniform of a class she cannot fully leave. Her obligation to protect the town is not rhetorical; it is the duty her house has sworn. She has always had "other reasons" for doing what she does. The reveal does not resolve her; it gives her context.

The matchmaking arc makes explicit what the Keele scene only hinted at. Darkness does not want to be married off, not even to a perfect man. Walther is handsome, talented, hardworking, charitable—in her own words, flawless, and that is exactly why she objects to him. "Which part am I unhappy about? Everything!" She does not want a perfect husband. She wants a degraded, lecherous, exploitative fantasy of a man, one who will chain her and waste her and murmur to her body. But underneath the joke—and the joke is very funny—the real objection is simpler and more dangerous to the social order: she does not want to stop being an adventurer. She would rather be disowned than retired. She says so directly: she will live a difficult life, take on impossible jobs, fail and be captured and abused, and she wants a life like that. Her masochistic fantasy can also be heard as a refusal of the fate her class has assigned her. It turns the bartering of her body into something she can imagine choosing. She would rather choose her own catastrophe than accept a safe one.

Her father is not a villain. He is a widower who spoiled her, who always kept her freedom in mind, who rejoices when she finally seems to accept a match. He tells Kazuma that she used to pray every day at the church of Eris for comrades to adventure with, until one day she came home happy because she had met a female thief named Chris. The story is tender and slightly awful: a lonely girl praying for friends, saved by adventure, now being asked to give it up. The party becomes the one place where she can be a comrade rather than a daughter, a marriageable asset, or a title. Darkness loves her father—she buys him souvenirs, worries over his illness, calls him "dear father"—and she still wants to "beat down my father every time he arranges a session." The conflict is not between good and evil but between two forms of love, one of which would smother her and one of which she cannot fully articulate.

Her friendship with Chris matters enormously, and the text is careful about it without narrating it directly. Chris is the friend Darkness prayed for. Chris is the one who names her "Darkness," a name that is both a joke about her emotional affect and a shield against the name that would otherwise define her. When Darkness prays for comrades, Chris is the answer. When Darkness meets Kazuma's party recruitment notice, she is newly alone after Chris has gone off to be useful elsewhere. The bond between them is old, teasing, physical—Chris throws a rock at her head to demonstrate Lurk and gets chased down in a barrel—and it is also one of the first relationships the books let us see her choose for herself, the love of a friend who saw her as a person rather than a position. In the final volume, when Darkness realizes that Chris and Kazuma have been keeping a secret from her, she does not cry out against the crime. She seizes them by the temples and demands to know why they did not tell her. "If you just told me about it clearly from the start," she says, "I would've definitely helped the both of you." The wound is not moral but relational. They did not trust her. And hence she was, again, the one excluded.

Her relationship with Kazuma is the long, slow, unstable center of her arc. From the start, she treats his harshness as a feature: begging for vicious lectures, reinterpreting his accidental cruelties as skill, asking to be his shield. But the relationship is never one-directional. When Kazuma dies, she is found on one knee holding his hand in prayer, and she hugs him wordlessly when he wakes. She defends him against drunkards. She saves him from execution by revealing her noble status and offering herself to a lecherous landlord. When the princess calls him a con man, Darkness demands she retract it and apologize, slaps her, and takes a sword to the wrist rather than let him be humiliated. She has, by this point, been reduced to a joke by him dozens of times, and she still chooses his dignity over her own safety.

Kazuma, for his part, sees more of her contradictions than most, and that understanding often sharpens into cruelty. He is sometimes too good at guessing what she hates and not good enough at guessing what she wants. He knows she will not shirk just before the last line. He knows her name is Lalatina and uses it to humiliate her. He knows she wants to be used and refuses to let her enjoy it when she asks. And he also knows—this is the crucial thing—that there are places in her where the performance stops. After the prayer monologue is overheard, after she is reduced to "the one who contributed the least," after he calls her body "slutty" and she covers herself and blinks back tears, he sees the real boundary. Her masochism is not infinite. It is a costume she puts on with full knowledge, and beneath it there is a private, easily wounded self that still yearns for something she cannot name.

Part of her attraction to Kazuma is that he, unlike Walther or the suitors or the nobles, does not treat her as a prize to be won or protected. He treats her as a limb of the party, a shield with a personality, someone to be insulted and relied upon. He sees her as both useful and ridiculous, and both of those recognitions are, for her, a form of being seen. When she says she prefers "an ordinary guy… for example, someone like you," she is not joking, or not only joking. She wants to be ordinary, which for her means being a person rather than a position. Kazuma offers her a version of that—a version, because he also reduces her, leers at her, uses her, and retreats from her vulnerability. But he is one of the few people in her life who knows she is both Lalatina and Darkness, and who still, in his grudging, exasperated way, chooses Darkness.

The choice, when it comes, is not a choice between the two names but a failure of them both. She leaves. She goes to the landlord's house after the Hydra is dead, and then sends a letter resigning from the party. The letter is formal, grateful, and final. It says she can no longer meet them. It says she is sorry. It says she carries something as a noble. And it leaves the reader, like Kazuma, staring at the pieces of a snapped penknife.

Why does she leave? The text does not fully say, and any answer must be built from fragments. Vanir tells her she bears "the sign of destruction." Alderp has been plotting to use her as a marriage pawn and worse. Her father is unwell, and she has been visiting him. She has been hunting the Kowloon Hydra with an urgency that is not noble duty, exactly, but something more desperate. She refuses to explain. "Protecting the people is the duty of the Dustiness house," she says, but the Hydra is not attacking the people. She is attacking it. The language of obligation does not settle the private urgency behind the hunt.

Vanir says she will think everything can be solved by sacrificing herself. The prophecy is one of the clearest warnings about the conflict she is entering. Her life repeatedly brings self-sacrifice close to self-destruction. She wants to be used, and she wants to matter. She wants to protect, and she wants to be overpowered. She wants to be chosen, and she wants to be the one choosing. In the end, she chooses to be the sacrifice, the noble thing, the shield. And in doing so she becomes, for the first time, entirely alone, and perilously close to the worst reading of herself.

Before she leaves, she stands before the town in the rain of monster blood and says, "I learned once again how much I like the people in town. Thanks to them, I found my way again. There's nothing to be afraid of, nothing to regret." It is a moment of hard-won serenity, and it is immediately followed by her disappearance. The serenity is not false. For a moment, public service and private desire no longer pull apart: the battle was a public service, and it was also, for her, the most direct confrontation with danger she has ever chosen. But the moment cannot settle what waits beyond the battle. The political world does not disappear because she has killed a monster. The landlord is still waiting. Her father is still failing. Her house is still hers.

Her masochism, I think, is not simply the opposite of her nobility. The books make it difficult to say where one ends and the other begins. A life shaped by being a shield—for the house, for the kingdom, for the princess—may have given her appetite a language, but it does not explain it away. She has grown up with marriage as political exchange; in fantasy, that exchange returns as degradation she can desire. She has been taught to put others first, and sacrifice has become entangled with appetite. Whether duty formed desire or merely gave it a stage is not something the books settle. The knight and the pervert are not two souls in one body. They are the same soul, half-aware of its own shape.

The text keeps returning to the moments when her masochism fails to cover something, when the costume slips and a more tender, more embarrassing self is visible. She prays for the dead and is mortified to be heard. She offers to be interrogated in prison and then insists her body is still pure. She lets the Axis cultists stone her but refuses to remove the Eris amulet. She will degrade herself, but she will not cheapen what her house stands for. She will let a man tie her up, but she will not let him call her a woman who seduces. She will strip before a princess, but she will not forgive being tricked into stripping before Kazuma. Each boundary names a value she cannot give up, even for the pleasure of losing.

The danger, and the tragedy, is that her values demand sacrifice, and she is very good at sacrificing. She throws herself into the Destroyer's path, into Vanir's possession, into the Hydra's stomach. She offers herself again and again as the one who will be hurt so that others are not. And each time, the text lets us see that this is both heroism and desire, that the two are impossible to separate. When she holds the Hydra's body off Kazuma and murmurs about dying together, she is fulfilling her duty and enjoying the pressure. When she asks to be left behind to be ravaged, she is offering a tactical advantage. When she finally leaves the party, she is, she believes, doing the right thing—and doing it in exactly the wrong way, alone.

The letter breaks Kazuma. He cannot eat. He snaps his knife. He refuses replacement after replacement, because none of them are her. In her absence, everyone finally says aloud what they could not say when she was present: she was necessary. Not because she could hit anything. Not because she was rich or noble. Because she was the body that stood between them and death, and because she did it with a human face. The party without her is not a party. It is a group of people waiting for the shield to come back.

And so the portrait of her must be a portrait of absence as much as presence. She is gone for much of her own story—fulfilling a promise, visiting her father, hunting her Hydra. She is silent at crucial moments. She is a beautiful lady in an expensive dress, and no one recognizes her. She is a name on a letter. She is the one who did not come back tonight. We know her best by what remains when she leaves.

But she is also, insistently, present. She is the one who asks the practical questions no one wants to ask. She is the one who carries the heavy pack. She is the one who strips off her armor to keep it safe before facing a slime. She is the one who says, of a woman who ran away with an undead mage, that her fugitive life was blessed. She is the one who, asked to do something impossible, says she cannot hit anything but is very good at being a shield. She is the one who will not bow to a monster out of fear, even if no one is watching.

What she wants, I think, is to be allowed to want. To want danger, to want pain, to want degradation, to want love—without being reduced to a function, a name, a body, or a shield. She wants to be chosen by someone who knows she is all of these things and still chooses her. Kazuma nearly becomes that person. The party nearly becomes that place. And then the world, which is always waiting, calls her back to her obligations, and she goes.

One line gathers much of her arc without resolving it. It is a joke she makes while dying. "We'll die… Together…" she murmurs, pinned under the Hydra with Kazuma beneath her. It is at once a wish for a shared death, a masochistic reverie, and a promise. She will not let him go. She will not let herself be separated from the person who, for a moment, made her feel like both a Crusader and a woman, both a shield and a self. The line is funny, and it is terrible, and it is her to the bone.

The uncertainty remains. I cannot say whether she loves Kazuma or merely cannot bear to be left out. I cannot say whether her departure is noble duty or masochist self-destruction. I cannot say whether she knows. I think she does not. I think that is the point of her. She is a woman who has been told what she is for so long that she has learned to enjoy the telling, and to echo it, and to crave the echo—while still, somewhere beneath all the noise, holding a small, unarticulated hope that someone will see through it and call her by her true name. Not Dustiness. Not Lalatina. Not the super masochist, not the top-tier tank, not the daughter of the national chief adviser.

Just Darkness. The name Chris gave her. The name the party refuses to trade for any noble title. The name she signs only when she resigns.

She is, in the end, a knight who cannot hit anything, a shield who wishes she were a sword, a pervert who prays, a noble who longs for the road, and a friend who cannot stand to be left out and nevertheless leaves in silence. She is always, somehow, both too much and insufficient—too tough for the joke to be entirely comfortable, too vulnerable for the heroism to be entirely clean. The story cannot resolve her, and does not. She remains, like her own hidden meanings, like her own off-page suffering, partly unknowable.

And that may be the most honest thing about her. She asks, at the end, not to be seen, but to be with. To be used is not the same as to be known; to be known is the terrifying thing. When Kazuma and the others come looking for her, she is behind a gate, in the landlord's house, writing a letter. She is, for once, not standing in front of anything. She is asking to be released. And the reader, like the party, is left with the sense that what costs her most may be the thing she cannot say out loud.

She leaves a hole in the shape of a person. That shape is Darkness. To paint her is to paint the hole.
