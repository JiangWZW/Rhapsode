# Subplot lifecycle — craft research

**Question:** WHEN and HOW should Rhapsode start, advance, and end off-stage storylines ("threads")?

This page collects findings from three domains — human writing craft, tabletop RPG design, and interactive narrative systems — and synthesizes them into actionable principles. The 06-10 episode proposed a mechanical architecture (shared pool, LOD, importance, autonomous narrator); this page supplies the **dramaturgical theory** that should govern the decisions that architecture makes.

---

## Part 1 — Human writing craft

### 1.1 Robert McKee — *Story* (1997)

McKee identifies four functions a subplot can serve relative to the main plot:

1. **Contradict** the controlling idea — show the opposite thesis (e.g., main plot argues "love conquers all," subplot argues "love destroys").
2. **Resonate** with the controlling idea — reinforce it from a different angle or different characters.
3. **Complicate** the main plot — create obstacles, diversions, or time pressure that make the central goal harder.
4. **Set up** the climax — plant information, relationships, or emotional stakes the main plot's ending depends on.

On timing, McKee is clear: subplots exist **in service of the main plot**. They should be introduced when the main plot *needs* them (complication, breathing room, thematic depth) and resolved before or during the main climax — never after, because a late-resolving subplot steals energy from the ending.

### 1.2 John Truby — *The Anatomy of Story* (2007)

Truby's key contribution is the **scene weave**: the technique of interleaving subplot beats with main-plot beats so that each scene serves **multiple narrative functions simultaneously**. His method:

1. Lay out each subplot as its own timeline of beats.
2. Look for **crossover points** — moments where one scene can advance both the main plot and a subplot (shared characters, shared locations, thematic juxtaposition).
3. **Cross-cut** between subplots at these points, so the audience experiences contrast and comparison rather than separate stories told in sequence.

Truby insists that every scene should serve at least two purposes. A subplot beat that only advances its own thread, with no connection to the main plot or another subplot, is a candidate for cutting.

### 1.3 Blake Snyder — *Save the Cat* (2005)

Snyder's beat sheet provides the most prescriptive timing for subplot introduction. In a standard 110-page screenplay:

| Beat | Page / % | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **B Story** | p. 30 / 22% | The subplot **kicks in** — typically a new character who embodies the theme and helps the protagonist transform. |
| **Fun and Games** | pp. 30—5 / 20—0% | The "promise of the premise" — main plot and B story interleave. |
| **Midpoint** | p. 55 / 50% | Stakes raised; false victory or false defeat. Subplot often delivers key information here. |
| **All Is Lost** | p. 75 / 75% | Protagonist's lowest point. Subplot may deliver the blow or be part of the wreckage. |
| **Dark Night of the Soul** | pp. 75—5 / 75—0% | Protagonist processes loss. **B Story delivers "new information"** — the insight needed for the final push. |
| **Break into Three** | p. 85 / 85% | Subplot's thematic lesson is internalized; protagonist acts on it. |

The structural lesson: **the subplot (B Story) is introduced at 22%, delivers its key payload at 75—0%, and is substantially resolved before the finale.** This is the most concrete published answer to "when."

### 1.4 James Scott Bell — *Plot & Structure* (2004)

Bell's framework centers on **two doorways of no return** and a **mirror moment**:

- **Disturbance** — something disrupts the protagonist's world (before 20%).
- **Doorway #1** — the protagonist commits irrevocably to the conflict (at 20%). Subplots should be seeded before or at this point.
- **Mirror moment** — at the exact midpoint (50%), the protagonist looks inward and confronts who they are. Subplots often crystallize the mirror moment by providing the character comparison that forces self-recognition.
- **Doorway #2** — at 75%, options narrow, forcing the final confrontation. Subplots should be resolved or converging by now.

Bell's LOCK method says each subplot must have its own **Lead, Objective, Confrontation, Knockout** — it is a complete story, just shorter. Subplots can be **serial** (one resolves before the next starts) or **parallel** (running simultaneously), but each must work on its own terms.

### 1.5 K.M. Weiland — *Creating Character Arcs* (2016)

Weiland's framework ties subplots to character transformation via **the Lie the character believes**:

- Every protagonist holds a deeply held misconception (the Lie), rooted in a painful past experience (the Ghost).
- Subplots should **challenge the Lie** by providing characters and situations that embody arguments for and against it.
- A subplot functions as a **thematic mirror** — it shows the protagonist (and audience) what happens if the Lie is embraced vs. rejected, through supporting characters who represent different stances.
- Subplot can embody a **push** (toward transformation), a **pull** (back to the old way), or a **temptation** (a distraction).

The timing implication: subplot beats should fire when the protagonist's relationship to the Lie needs to shift. The early subplot beats establish the Lie's grip; the midpoint beats crack it; the late beats force a choice.

### 1.6 Nancy Kress — *Beginnings, Middles & Ends* (1999)

Kress frames every story as a **war**: two sides, something at stake, struggle, and a decisive outcome. Subplots are **secondary fronts** in the same war.

Gene Wolfe's advice to Kress, which she credits as career-defining:

> "Have two different things going on in a story and then at the end have the two things impact each other."

Kress's practical rules:
- Subplots should **complicate the protagonist's main goal** — not merely exist alongside it.
- As long as a subplot remains connected to the main conflict, it enriches the story. When it drifts, it **derails** the pace.
- The writer's job in the middle is **alertness** — constant self-monitoring that every scene serves the story.

### 1.7 Brandon Sanderson — lectures (BYU, 2020+)

Sanderson structures subplots as **nested arcs** using his **Promise / Progress / Payoff** framework:

- **Promise**: seed the subplot — introduce the question, the tension, or the relationship. The audience now expects something.
- **Progress**: advance it — show the subplot changing, escalating, or developing. Each progress beat should also advance the main story.
- **Payoff**: resolve it — answer the question, deliver the emotional beat, close the arc.

Sanderson's emphasis: **every promise must eventually pay off, and every payoff must have been promised.** An unfired Chekhov's gun is a broken contract. A payoff with no setup feels like deus ex machina.

### 1.8 Film Crit Hulk — *Screenwriting 101* (2013)

Film Crit Hulk argues against rigid three-act structure in favor of **five acts** (following Shakespeare), and makes a key observation about subplots:

> "A story is a multifaceted thing. If you want to structure your story, remember to have both act structure for the main plot and act structure for each of your characters' personality developments. By having all these varying structures, each with their own beats, it creates a constant sense of 'moving forward.'"

Each subplot and each character arc should have its own **independent beat structure** that interlocks with the main plot's structure. When these multiple structures' turning points align at different times, the story always has something in motion — there are no dead stretches.

### 1.9 Stephen King — *On Writing* (2000)

King famously distrusts plotting and identifies as a "situationist":

> "I distrust plot for two reasons: first, because our lives are largely plotless, even when you add in all our reasonable precautions and careful planning; and second, because I believe plotting and the spontaneity of real creation aren't compatible."

His method: **start with a situation (a "what-if"), add characters, and narrate — watching what they do.** Subplots are not planned; they emerge when characters grow enough to "begin to influence the course of the story instead of the other way around." Theme and patterns are identified in the second draft, then strengthened:

> "Good fiction always begins with story and progresses to theme; it almost never begins with theme and progresses to story."

> "When you write a story, you're telling yourself the story. When you rewrite, your main job is taking out all the things that are not the story."

The implication for Rhapsode: King's process is the **pure gardener** approach — let subplots emerge from character behavior, then prune ruthlessly in revision. This is the opposite of Snyder's prescriptive beat sheet. Both methods produce great fiction. For an automated system, King's approach maps to the "emergent subplot from character agency" model: if characters have autonomous intentions (the 06-10 episode's "forward intention"), subplots arise naturally from collisions between those intentions. The system's job is then editorial — recognizing which emergent threads are interesting and which are noise.

### 1.10 George R.R. Martin — the gardener vs. the architect

Martin coined the most famous articulation of the two creative modes:

> "There are two types of writers: architects and gardeners. [...] The gardeners dig a hole, plant a seed and water it with their blood and their tears and their sweat — and they hope that something comes up. They have a general idea — they know whether they planted an acorn or a tomato plant. But there's lots of surprises."

> "I know the ultimate end of the series and I know the fates of all of the principal characters, but there's a lot of minor characters and other details that I find along the way. For me, both as a reader and a writer, it's about the journey, not the ultimate destination."

The failure mode of pure gardening is visible in Martin's own work: subplots grew "like weeds," what was planned as a trilogy ballooned to seven books, and *The Winds of Winter* remains unfinished after 14+ years. The lesson: **emergent subplots need pruning.** A system that generates threads freely but never retires them will sprawl exactly as ASOIAF did. Martin knows the endpoint and the major beats; the minor subplots are discovered. For Rhapsode, this confirms the need for a **destination** (the scenario's authored ending conditions) combined with **emergent middles** (threads generated by character behavior) and **active pruning** (unused threads retired).

### 1.11 Aaron Sorkin — intention and obstacle

Sorkin reduces drama to its most atomic unit:

> "Drama is someone wanting something badly with something formidable standing in their way."

> "Somebody wants something and something is standing in their way of getting it. [...] The tactics that your character uses to overcome the obstacle, whether they succeed or fail — that's going to be your play or your movie or your episode of television. And I can't begin to write until I know what someone wants and what's stopping them from getting it."

Sorkin explicitly admits that plot is his "Achilles heel" and that intention-and-obstacle is the "life raft" that keeps him from writing "30 pages of snappy dialogue where nothing is happening."

For Rhapsode: every subplot beat must be expressible as **someone wants X, Y stands in the way.** If a beat can't be expressed this way, it isn't dramatic — it's exposition. This is the beat-level test that complements Mamet's scene-level test (below) and Parker/Stone's sequence-level test (but/therefore).

### 1.12 David Mamet — the three questions

Mamet's memo to his writers on *The Unit* is one of the most cited documents in professional screenwriting:

> "EVERY SCENE MUST BE DRAMATIC. THAT MEANS: THE MAIN CHARACTER MUST HAVE A SIMPLE, STRAIGHTFORWARD, PRESSING NEED WHICH IMPELS HIM OR HER TO SHOW UP IN THE SCENE."

The three litmus questions, applied to every scene:

1. **Who wants what?**
2. **What happens if they don't get it?**
3. **Why now?**

> "THE JOB OF THE DRAMATIST IS TO MAKE THE AUDIENCE WONDER WHAT HAPPENS NEXT. NOT TO EXPLAIN TO THEM WHAT JUST HAPPENED, OR TO SUGGEST TO THEM WHAT HAPPENS NEXT."

And the corollary: each scene must end with the character **either thwarted or educated that another way exists.** This failure propels us into the next scene.

Mamet's danger signals: "Any time two characters are talking about a third, the scene is a crock of shit. Any time any character is saying to another 'as you know,' the scene is a crock of shit."

For Rhapsode: **every subplot beat the system generates must pass Mamet's three questions.** A beat about "trackers are moving through the woods" fails — who wants what? A beat about "trackers want to find the player's trail, but the rain is washing it away, and they must find it before dark or lose the scent" passes. The LLM prompt for generating subplot beats should enforce these three questions.

### 1.13 Vince Gilligan — painting into corners (Breaking Bad)

Gilligan's Breaking Bad writers' room process is the most thoroughly documented example of professional subplot management:

**The method:** The room uses corkboards, index cards, and markers. They "break" each episode beat-by-beat (teaser + four acts). The room starts each season by brainstorming **big ideas** — compelling images or situations — and pinning them to the board, then working backward to figure out how to get there.

> "We come up with an image — for instance, the Denny's scene where Walt buys a Cadillac with an M60 machine gun in the trunk. We come up with these scenes, my six writers and I, that intrigue us and resonate with us, and then we say, 'OK, what does that mean? Where does that go?'"

> "We had some broad-stroke ideas of how and when that would pay off. But we knew surprisingly little about how exactly and in what precise detail it would pay off."

**The discipline:** The one inviolable principle is **character-driven causation.** The writers constantly ask "where is the character's head at?" rather than forcing predetermined plot points. Gilligan:

> "The best writing is to let the characters tell you where they want to go and tell your story as organically as possible."

**The lesson for Rhapsode:** The Breaking Bad process is a hybrid — architects at the season/macro level (they know the destination: "Mr. Chips to Scarface"), gardeners at the episode/micro level (they discover how to get there). They deliberately create obligations (the M60 in the trunk) without knowing the payoff, then let the constraint force creative solutions. This is the **plant-first-payoff-later** pattern, but with honest uncertainty about what the payoff will be. For a system, this means: authored scenario goals set the destination; the LLM generates the beat-by-beat path; and unresolved plants (Foreshadowed nodes) create constructive pressure toward eventual payoffs.

### 1.14 Ursula K. Le Guin — crowding and leaping (*Steering the Craft*, 1998/2015)

Le Guin's "crowding and leaping" addresses pacing at the sentence and scene level:

> "By crowding, I mean keeping the story full, always full of what's happening in it; keeping it moving, not slackening, and wandering into irrelevancies; keeping it interconnected with itself, rich with echoes forward and backward."

> "But leaping is just as important. What you leap over is what you leave out. And what you leave out is infinitely more than what you leave in. There's got to be white space around the word, silence around the voice."

> "Everything that is crowded in should be in focus — part of the central focus of the story. And every leap should be along the trajectory — following the shape and movement of the whole."

Le Guin also pushes back against the primacy of conflict-driven plotting:

> "Plot is not superior to story and not even necessary to it."

Her definition of story: "a narrative of events which moves through time or implies the passage of time, and which involves **change**." Not conflict, not three acts — change.

For Rhapsode: subplot beats should be **crowded** (rich with implication, connected to other threads) and the spaces between them should be **leaped** (don't narrate the boring travel; jump to the next moment of change). Off-stage subplot beats are inherently leaps — "over three days the trackers close the distance" is a leap that implies the crowded details without narrating them.

### 1.15 Orson Scott Card — the MICE quotient

Card's MICE quotient (*Characters and Viewpoint*, 1988) classifies stories by their dominant structural element:

| Type | Focus | Begins when... | Ends when... |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Milieu** | Setting/world | Character enters a strange place | Character exits (or the place is understood) |
| **Idea** | A question | A question is posed | The question is answered |
| **Character** | Internal transformation | A character becomes dissatisfied with their role | They find a new role or accept the old one |
| **Event** | Disrupted status quo | Something goes wrong with the world | Order is restored or permanently changed |

Every story contains all four, but one dominates and determines the **start and end conditions.**

Mary Robinette Kowal (Hugo winner, co-host of *Writing Excuses*) extended MICE with a critical **nesting rule**: threads opened first should be closed last (FILO — first in, last out). If you open an Event thread, then a Character thread, you resolve Character first, then Event. Breaking this order creates an unsatisfying feeling of unresolved business.

For Rhapsode: each subplot thread has a MICE type. A "tracker pursuit" is an Event thread (disrupted status quo → resolution). "Maren's hidden wound" is a Character thread (dissatisfaction → acceptance or change). "What's in the crypt?" is an Idea thread (question → answer). The nesting rule constrains resolution order: the last-opened thread should resolve first.

## Part 2 — Causal logic and emotional pacing

### 2.1 The "But / Therefore" rule — Parker & Stone (South Park)

Matt Stone and Trey Parker's rule, from their NYU lecture, is the single most-cited piece of modern craft advice:

> "We can take the beats of your outline. And if the words 'and then' belong between those beats, you've got something pretty boring. What should happen between every beat is either the word **therefore** or **but**."

- **Therefore** = logical consequence (this happened, so the next thing follows).
- **But** = complication or reversal (this happened, but something unexpected intervened).
- **And then** = mere sequence, no causation. This is the sign of a broken story.

Applied to subplots: a subplot beat should fire **when it can be connected to the current moment by "but" or "therefore."** If the only connection is "and then" — a mere coincidence of timing — the beat isn't ready.

Amy Stewart (novelist) adds the practical caveat:

> "If you have more than one plot line going, do a different BUT/THEREFORE for each subplot. When you hook the whole thing back together again, there's just no way around the fact that you're going to have some MEANWHILEs in there."

Some subplot beats *are* just MEANWHILEs with no causal link to the main plot. That's acceptable but should be rare and brief.

### 2.2 Dan Harmon's Story Circle

Harmon distills the Hero's Journey into eight steps:

1. **Comfort** — a character is in a zone of comfort
2. **Want** — but they want something
3. **Unfamiliar** — they enter an unfamiliar situation
4. **Adapt** — they adapt to it
5. **Get** — they get what they wanted
6. **Pay** — they pay a heavy price for it
7. **Return** — they return to their familiar situation
8. **Change** — having changed

Each step should connect to the next by "but" or "therefore." A subplot can have its own compressed story circle. The main plot and subplots' circles interleave, with their beats landing at different times to maintain constant forward motion.

### 2.3 The Story Spine — Kenn Adams / Pixar

The Story Spine is the **minimal generative structure** for a narrative arc:

```
Once upon a time... [status quo]
Every day... [routine]
Until one day... [inciting incident]
Because of that... [consequence 1]
Because of that... [consequence 2]
Because of that... [consequence 3]
Until finally... [climax]
And ever since then... [new normal]
```

The "because of that" chain is the **minimal generative structure for a subplot**. A subplot can be fully specified as:

```
Inciting incident: Voss sends trackers to find the Player.
Because of that: Trackers enter the eastern woods.
Because of that: Trackers find the Player's trail.
Because of that: Trackers follow the trail toward town.
Until finally: Trackers arrive and confront the Player.
```

Each "because of that" is one beat. The chain is the subplot's plan, generated once and executed beat-by-beat. The chain is inspectable, revisable, and has a definite end.

### 2.4 Robin Laws — beat types and emotional oscillation

Laws' *Hamlet's Hit Points* provides **nine beat types**, each with a defined emotional effect:

| Beat type         | Category    | What it does                                      | Emotional direction            |
| ----------------- | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| **Procedural**    | Foundation  | Character overcomes a practical obstacle          | Up (success) or down (failure) |
| **Dramatic**      | Foundation  | Character seeks emotional concession from another | Up (granted) or down (refused) |
| **Pipe**          | Information | Plants information for later use                  | Lateral (no resolution yet)    |
| **Question**      | Information | Raises a question in the audience's mind          | Lateral (suspense)             |
| **Reveal**        | Information | Answers a previously planted question             | Up or down                     |
| **Commentary**    | Flourish    | Pauses to reflect on theme                        | Lateral                        |
| **Anticipation**  | Flourish    | Looks forward to a coming event                   | Up                             |
| **Gratification** | Flourish    | Revels in a pleasant stimulus                     | Up                             |
| **Bringdown**     | Flourish    | Lowers mood, accentuates loss                     | Down                           |

The core pacing principle: **a well-paced story oscillates between hope (up) and fear (down).** Lateral beats provide breathing room. Laws:

> "Are players having an easy time of it lately? Drop in a hard move. Are players having a lot of trouble recently? Give them a break."

A subplot beat should be **selected to serve this oscillation**, not fired on its own internal clock. If the last few beats have been fearful, the next should be hopeful or lateral. The beat type classification gives the vocabulary: "fire a **reveal** from the tracker subplot because the main plot just had a **gratification** and needs a downturn."

### 2.5 Plant / payoff timing — the Rule of Threes

Professional screenwriting consensus on plant-to-payoff timing:

> "If you have a gag of some kind, you'll need to properly introduce it, for it to pay off. On top of this you will usually remind the audience at least once, just so they'll remember when it comes into play. So the rule of three is: **1. Setup, 2. Reminder, 3. Payoff.**"

The timing depends on gap length:
- **Short gap** (within a scene or adjacent scenes): no reminder needed.
- **Long gap** (across acts / many turns): at least one reminder beat.
- **Very long gap** (across a whole story): multiple reminders, increasingly specific.

Industrial Scripts adds the crucial authoring constraint:

> "Don't set up a plant and then search for the payoff. The plant and payoff have to come together, then be separated by the narrative development."

Subplots should be designed as **plant+payoff pairs from the start**, not as open-ended threads that "might converge." The system's job is to deliver the reminder beats and control the timing — not to decide *whether* the payoff happens.

## Part 3 — Tabletop RPG design systems

### 3.1 Apocalypse World — fronts and countdown clocks (Baker, 2010)

The most influential RPG mechanic for off-stage subplot advancement. A **front** is a collection of linked threats, each with:

- A **type** and **impulse** (e.g., "Warlord — impulse: to own and control")
- **Stakes questions** — open questions the GM genuinely doesn't know the answer to ("Will the water supply hold?")
- A **countdown clock** — a 6-segment progression from normalcy to catastrophe

The countdown clock is both **descriptive** (when listed events happen in fiction, advance the clock) and **prescriptive** (when you advance the clock mechanically, the listed events happen). Baker:

> "A countdown clock is a reminder to you as MC that your threats have impulse, direction, plans, intentions, the will to sustain action and to respond coherently to others'."

Key design properties:
- **Clocks advance when the players fail or ignore the threat.** Player inaction has consequences.
- **Anything past the 9:00 mark cannot be reversed, only mitigated.** This creates genuine urgency.
- **Clocks are hidden from players.** The players experience the fictional symptoms, not the mechanic.
- The GM principle is **"think offscreen too"** — threats have their own momentum independent of player attention.

For Rhapsode: countdown clocks are the purest model of a "ticking clock" subplot. The tracker pursuit has a 6-step clock: idle → dispatched → enter woods → find trail → close in → arrive. Each player turn that doesn't address the trackers advances the clock. The clock is hidden (the player sees "broken branches" and "armed men heading east," not a progress bar). Past a threshold, arrival becomes inevitable.

### 3.2 DramaSystem / Hillfolk — the petitioner/granter economy (Laws, 2012)

Laws' DramaSystem mechanizes dramatic scenes with a token economy:

> "One character is the **petitioner**, who seeks emotional gratification from a second character, the **granter**. [...] If the petition is willingly granted, the granter earns a drama token. If the granter refuses, the petitioner earns the token."

The system solves a problem: **characters in RPGs almost never yield.** When two characters disagree, both players dig in, stalemate ensues, and nothing moves. The token economy incentivizes both granting and refusing by giving tokens to the "loser" of each exchange. Tokens accumulate, and with enough you can *force* a concession.

Laws' Game of Thrones analysis of one episode demonstrates the system at work:
- Cersei refuses three petitions → three tokens flow to petitioners → those characters accumulate narrative power for their next move
- Daenerys petitions the dragons for reconciliation, they refuse → she gains a token → more power for the next attempt

For subplot lifecycle: **a subplot advances through a sequence of dramatic petitions (requests between characters) that are either granted or refused.** Each exchange changes the power balance and creates the next beat. A subplot is "active" when its characters have unresolved petitions. It ends when the final petition is resolved. This gives structure to what a "beat" actually is — not an arbitrary narration, but a specific dramatic exchange with a winner and a loser.

### 3.3 Sly Flourish — "ten secrets" preparation (Shea, 2018)

*Return of the Lazy Dungeon Master* provides the most practical model for preparing subplot material without over-committing:

> "Write down ten tweet-sized secrets. These secrets may never come into play. They don't become real until the PCs discover them. They might change. They might disappear."

Key properties:
- **Abstracted from discovery method** — you don't plan *how* the player finds it, only *what* it is. The method is improvised at the table.
- **Fluid until revealed** — an unrevealed secret can be rewritten, discarded, or carried forward.
- **Short** — one or two sentences. Not a scene, not a beat, just a fact.

On discarding unused material:

> "Your good ideas will come back. The important secrets will stay on your lists week after week until they get revealed. The world can change. You might have better ideas later. If we keep our secrets they become links in a chain that anchor us down to a world that need not be."

For Rhapsode: this maps to `Node`s with `Foreshadowed` state. The scenario's seed nodes are "secrets" — facts about the world that exist but haven't been surfaced. They become threads only when the narrator introduces them. The system should maintain a pool of available hooks, not commit to when or how they surface, and discard or rewrite them if the story moves in a different direction.

### 3.4 The Alexandrian — Three Clue Rule and proactive nodes (Alexander)

Justin Alexander's Three Clue Rule:

> "For any conclusion you want the PCs to make, include at least three clues."

And the Inverse Three Clue Rule:

> "If the PCs have access to ANY three clues, they will reach at least ONE conclusion."

For subplot **starting**: a subplot should be discoverable via at least three independent paths. The relic thread shouldn't require the player to talk to Aldric specifically. It should be discoverable by: (1) noticing Aldric's nightly visits to the crypt, (2) finding a historical text about the relic, (3) overhearing soldiers speculate about why Harren wants Ashenmoor specifically.

Alexander's **proactive nodes** are directly relevant:

> "Proactive nodes are encounters that seek out the players — a police patrol, an assassin, a messenger. They inject dynamic movement or provide fresh leads if the investigation hits a dead end."

A subplot that isn't being discovered through the player's own exploration should have **proactive delivery** — the subplot comes to the player, rather than waiting for the player to find it. The trackers don't wait for the player to investigate the woods; they *approach the player's location*. This is the "push" mechanism the 06-09 episode diagnosed as missing.

### 3.5 TV writers' room structure — A/B/C stories

The television writers' room has the most battle-tested system for managing concurrent subplots:

- **A story** — the main plot, gets ~60% of screen time.
- **B story** — the primary subplot, ~25%. Usually the "emotional" or "thematic" thread.
- **C story** ("runner") — a lightweight comedic or atmospheric thread, ~15%. Often resolved within the episode.

Practical rules:
- **Each story has its own beat sheet** — beginning, middle, end, tracked on index cards.
- Stories are **intercut** so that no single thread dominates for too long.
- The **showrunner** maintains overall vision; individual writers may own individual threads.
- **Pacing timelines** (literal physical timelines on a wall) track where each thread is in its arc.
- **"Knowing when to drop a thread"** — if a subplot isn't working, the room cuts it rather than forcing it. The footage already shot becomes a deleted scene.

### 3.6 Matt Colville — central tension and the villain journal (Running the Game)

Colville's *Running the Game* YouTube series (3M+ subscribers) provides two directly relevant tools:

**Central tension:** Every campaign needs a **single axis of conflict** around which all factions, NPCs, and subplots pivot. In his Iomandra-adjacent campaigns, this is usually a political power vacuum ("If Ajax the Invincible is removed from power, all nations go to war"). Subplots are not independent stories — they are different factions' responses to the same central tension. This ensures that no subplot is truly disconnected from the main story.

**The villain journal** (a "between sessions" prep tool): a three-line entry written from the antagonist's perspective between each session:

1. **Objective** — what the villain wants *this week*, not eventually.
2. **Move** — what concrete action they take to advance that objective.
3. **Tell** — what sign of this action reaches the wider world.

Every entry must do at least one of: advance a villain goal, escalate stakes, change the world state, or create a choice for the players.

Colville's core principle on player engagement:

> "If you want them to care about your world, that lore has to be relevant to the adventure. You have to present problems that can only be solved by caring about your lore."

For Rhapsode: the villain journal maps directly to the "Next Move" pattern — one sentence per NPC per turn, generating the off-stage beat. The three-line format (objective/move/tell) is a prompt template for off-stage subplot advancement. The "tell" line is the proactive delivery mechanism — the symptom the player sees.

### 3.7 Chris Perkins — the campaign as TV series (Dungeon Master Experience)

Perkins ran D&D's most famous long-running campaign (Iomandra, 2008—013) across two concurrent groups on Monday and Wednesday nights, and wrote a 100+ article column documenting his techniques.

His key structural insight: **treat the campaign like a TV series.** Each session is an "episode," groups of sessions form "seasons," and the campaign has an "episode guide" — a one-line summary per session that tracks the narrative arc:

> "I created the side trek to remind my players that there's far more going on in the world of Iomandra than 'the quest at hand.'"

On villains and subplots: Perkins' villains are "deeply, profoundly flawed" — they miscalculate, fall down, and suffer setbacks. This makes them exploitable by players and creates the material for subplot beats. The key observation: **a subplot advances when a villain's flaw creates an opportunity or a consequence**, not when a timer fires.

### 3.8 Brennan Lee Mulligan — volcanic worldbuilding and the shape of story (Dimension 20)

Mulligan, the most acclaimed actual-play GM of the current era, offers two principles:

**Volcanic worldbuilding:** Bring a world that has "tone" and "a big box of toys" but is deliberately **underdeveloped** in the areas the players haven't chosen to care about. Let player choices determine which areas get detailed. Don't pre-develop "every last square inch" at maximum resolution.

> "What I recommend is doing a lot of your worldbuilding based on the choices of your PCs. Keep your world a little bit volcanic when you bring it to your PCs."

**The shape of story vs. the straight line:** Mulligan's formulation of the GM's fundamental task:

> "[We are looking to] achieve the shape of a story while trying your hardest to go in a straight line."

Players want a straight line to their goals. The GM's job is to place obstacles that curve that line into a satisfying narrative shape — without the players feeling railroaded. Subplots are a primary tool for creating that curvature.

On mystery construction (from *Mice & Murder*): Mulligan built the season as an "amusement park" — clues and set pieces designed to be encountered in any order, each giving an exciting piece of information independent of sequence. This is Alexander's node-based design in practice at the highest production level.

### 3.9 Tabletop RPG game mastering — practical craft synthesis

From Sly Flourish, The Alexandrian, Colville, Perkins, Mulligan, and broader GM craft communities:

**When to introduce a subplot:**
- When the main plot has a natural lull or pause.
- When a player character's backstory creates an opening.
- When a "secret" (prepared fact) becomes relevant to the current situation.
- When the emotional direction needs a change (per Laws' oscillation principle).
- When the central tension creates a new factional response (Colville).

**How to introduce it:**
- Through an NPC who approaches the player (proactive delivery).
- Through environmental detail that raises a question.
- Through a consequence of a previous player action.
- Through a "meanwhile" narration between scenes.
- Through a villain's "tell" — the visible symptom of an off-screen move (Colville).

**When to advance it:**
- Each session, look at each active subplot and decide: does it need a beat this session? If the player hasn't engaged it in two sessions, either fire a proactive beat or shelve it.
- The **"Next Move" / villain journal pattern**: for every active NPC agenda, write one sentence — "What does this NPC do next?" The answer is the next subplot beat.
- When a villain's flaw creates an opportunity (Perkins).

**When to end it:**
- When the dramatic question is answered.
- When the player resolves it through action.
- When it converges with the main plot (the ideal ending — the subplot *becomes* the main plot for a moment).
- When it's no longer interesting — just stop mentioning it and let it fade. (Honest GM advice: some threads are dropped, and that's fine.)
- When the epilogue arrives — tie up loose ends, then "pull back the curtain" (Perkins).

## Part 4 — Interactive narrative systems

### 4.1 Storylets and quality-based narrative (Failbetter / Emily Short)

Emily Short's taxonomy of narrative architectures beyond branching:

**Quality-based narrative (QBN)** — stories structured around **storylets** (discrete, self-contained narrative modules) unlocked by **qualities** (numerical state variables). Failbetter Games (Fallen London) pioneered this. A storylet has:
- **Preconditions** — qualities that must be met for it to be available.
- **Content** — a paragraph or two of text, followed by a choice.
- **Effects** — quality changes that result from the choice.

**Salience-based narrative** — the system (not the player) selects the most relevant content from a pool based on current state. Each storylet is tagged with an "ideal" state; the system picks the storylet whose ideal state most closely matches the actual state. The most-constrained eligible storylet wins (specificity beats generality).

Short's key insight for Rhapsode:

> "In a QBN, the player is choosing which storylet to do next. In a salience system, the system is choosing which piece of content to serve next."

Rhapsode's subplot selection is inherently salience-based — the system (not the player) decides which off-stage thread to advance. The selection algorithm should pick the thread whose preconditions are most specifically met by the current world state.

### 4.2 Kreminski's storylet taxonomy (ICIDS 2018)

Max Kreminski and Noah Wardrip-Fruin identify four independent dimensions of storylet-based systems:

1. **Precondition type** — what gates a storylet's availability (qualities, location, time, narrative history).
2. **Repeatability** — can a storylet be experienced more than once?
3. **Internal structure** — is the content linear text, branching, or procedurally generated?
4. **Content selection architecture** — how the system chooses which storylet to present:
   - Player selection (QBN)
   - Random selection
   - Weighted random selection
   - Salience-based selection (most-constrained-first)
   - Pathfinding-based selection (toward a narrative goal)

For Rhapsode, subplot beats are storylets with: preconditions on WorldGraph state, non-repeatable, LLM-generated internal content, and salience-based selection (the system picks the beat whose preconditions are most specifically satisfied).

### 4.3 Felt / Starfreighter — story sifting for storylet selection (Kreminski et al.)

Felt is an academic tool that combines **story sifting** (pattern matching over narrative history) with **storylet selection**. Key mechanism:

- Each storylet has **sifting patterns** that query the chronicle of past events.
- A storylet becomes eligible when its patterns match sequences in the history.
- Selection uses weighted random among eligible storylets.
- Storylets can contain characters **reflecting on past events**, creating emergent narrative coherence.

This directly maps to Rhapsode's `WorldGraph`: storylet preconditions are graph queries ("there exists a node where Voss's trackers were dispatched AND no node where they were intercepted"), and the selection mechanism picks the most specific match.

### 4.4 Valve's dynamic dialogue — fuzzy pattern matching (Ruskin, GDC 2012)

Elan Ruskin's system for Left 4 Dead et al.:

- The game tracks **hundreds of facts** about the world state in a uniform database.
- A **rule database** contains thousands of possible dialogue lines, each with a set of fact-conditions.
- When a dialogue opportunity arises, the system evaluates all rules against the current state and selects the rule with the **most conditions satisfied** (most-specific-match-wins).
- Rules cascade from highly specific ("if character is Bill AND teammate just died AND health < 20% AND in the hospital level") to general ("if teammate just died").

This is salience-based selection applied to a shipped AAA game. The lesson for Rhapsode: subplot beat selection can use the same most-specific-match algorithm over WorldGraph queries, with no planning or AI needed for the selection itself — the intelligence is in authoring the conditions.

### 4.5 RimWorld — storyteller pacing cycles (Sylvester, 2013+)

RimWorld's AI storytellers manage procedural drama through explicit pacing cycles:

**Cassandra Classic** (the default):
- Begins on day 11.
- Alternates between **4.6-day "on" phases** (1— major threats, minimum 1.9 days apart) and **6-day "off" phases** (recovery).
- Threat difficulty scales with colony wealth, not time.
- Adapts to near-death situations: low wealth → smaller raids → recovery possible.

**Randy Random:**
- No fixed cycle; fires events at ~1.35/day average.
- **13-day maximum interval** — if no major threat has fired in 13 days, the next event is forced to be one.
- Threat size multiplied by 0.5×—.5× randomly, creating wilder variance.

The design lessons:
- **Explicit on/off cycles** prevent both monotony and exhaustion. Rhapsode subplot beats should have minimum spacing (don't fire two subplot beats back-to-back) and maximum intervals (don't let a subplot go silent for too long without either advancing or being shelved).
- **Wealth-based (state-based) scaling**, not time-based, keeps difficulty appropriate to the player's actual situation.
- **The "maximum interval" backstop** is the simplest possible "push" mechanism: if nothing has happened in N turns, force something. This is the safety net that prevents the 100%-pull failure mode the 06-09 episode diagnosed.

### 4.6 Façade — beat sequencing and tension arc (Mateas & Stern, 2005)

Façade's drama manager sequences **beats** (authored dramatic micro-scenes) by matching preconditions against world state and fitting them to an Aristotelian tension arc. Key mechanisms:

- Beats have **preconditions** (relationship values, story progress, character states) and **effects**.
- The drama manager maintains a **target tension curve** (rising, with a peak at the climax).
- Beat selection optimizes for: preconditions met AND beat's tension contribution fitting the target arc.

The lesson: subplot beat selection should consider not just *eligibility* (are preconditions met?) but also *dramatic need* (does this beat serve the current position on the tension arc?).

## Part 5 — Convergent principles

Across all three domains, the same fundamental structures appear. This section distills them.

### 5.1 A subplot is a causal chain of 3— beats

Every source agrees: a subplot is a **mini-story with its own beginning, middle, and end.** It has:

- A **dramatic question** (will the trackers find the player?)
- A **causal chain** of beats, each connected by "but" or "therefore"
- A **resolution** that answers the question
- A **MICE type** (Card): Milieu, Idea, Character, or Event — determining its start/end conditions

The minimum viable subplot is **3 beats**: setup, complication, resolution. A richer one has 4—. The Story Spine's "because of that" chain is the generative template.

Each beat must pass **Mamet's three questions**: Who wants what? What happens if they don't get it? Why now? And each beat must express **Sorkin's drive shaft**: a character wanting something badly, with something formidable in their way.

### 5.2 WHEN to start a subplot

Sources converge on five triggers:

| Trigger | Source |
|---|---|
| **The main plot needs complication or contrast** | McKee, Truby, Sanderson, Kress |
| **The emotional direction needs to change** (oscillation) | Laws, RimWorld pacing cycle |
| **The protagonist's arc needs a thematic mirror** | Weiland, Film Crit Hulk |
| **A maximum interval has elapsed** (nothing has happened in too long) | RimWorld, Apocalypse World clocks |
| **A character's situation becomes intolerable** (MICE Character trigger) | Card, King (character begins to "influence the course of the story") |

Structural constraints:
- **Introduce subplots early** (before the 20—5% mark per Snyder and Bell), because late introductions don't have time to develop.
- **Nest threads in FILO order** (Card/Kowal): the first thread opened should be the last to close. Open a new thread inside an existing one, not alongside it.
- **Start from a situation, not a theme** (King): the subplot should emerge from a concrete "what-if," not from an abstract desire to add depth.

### 5.3 HOW to start a subplot

| Method | Source |
|---|---|
| **Seed it with a plant** — introduce an element (fact, character, question) that promises a future payoff | Sanderson (Promise), Chekhov, Snyder (B Story) |
| **Provide multiple discovery paths** — at least three clues pointing to the subplot | Alexander (Three Clue Rule), Mulligan (amusement park design) |
| **Use proactive delivery** if the player hasn't discovered it organically | Alexander (proactive nodes), AW (fronts push toward players), Colville (villain "tell") |
| **Abstract from delivery method** — decide *what* the subplot IS, not *how* it's found | Sly Flourish (ten secrets), King ("situation first") |
| **Derive from the central tension** — the subplot is a faction's response to the same core conflict | Colville (central tension) |

### 5.4 WHEN to advance a subplot beat

| Rule | Source |
|---|---|
| **When the beat connects to the current moment by "but" or "therefore"** | Parker & Stone, Sorkin ("but, and then, except") |
| **When the emotional oscillation needs it** (the mood should change direction) | Laws (beat types) |
| **When the reminder gap is too long** — audience will forget the thread without a nudge | Rule of Threes (setup-eminder-ayoff) |
| **When the countdown clock ticks** — player inaction or failure advances the threat | Apocalypse World |
| **When salience peaks** — the beat's preconditions are most specifically met right now | Emily Short, Valve/Ruskin, Façade |
| **When the villain's flaw creates an opportunity** | Perkins (flawed villains), Colville (villain journal) |

Spacing constraints:
- **Minimum interval between subplot beats** (don't fire back-to-back; let the main plot breathe). RimWorld's Cassandra enforces 1.9 days between major threats.
- **Crowd the beats, leap between them** (Le Guin): each beat should be rich with implication; the spaces between beats should be boldly skipped.

### 5.5 WHEN to end a subplot

| Signal | Source |
|---|---|
| **The dramatic question is answered** | Every screenwriting source, Card (MICE end conditions) |
| **The subplot converges with the main plot** (the ideal ending) | Truby (scene weave), Wolfe via Kress |
| **The subplot's thematic payload is delivered** — the protagonist internalizes the lesson | Weiland, Snyder (Dark Night of the Soul) |
| **Before the main climax** — subplots should not outlive the main plot's final act | McKee, Snyder, Bell |
| **The premise is superseded** — world events killed the subplot's driving assumption | Apocalypse World (superseded fronts), Rhapsode Weaver (existing `check_group`) |
| **It's no longer interesting** — drop it and move on | TV writers' room, Sly Flourish, Martin (the "weed" problem — prune or sprawl) |
| **The MICE type's end condition is met** | Card: Milieu → exit the world; Idea → question answered; Character → new role accepted; Event → order restored |

### 5.6 HOW to end a subplot

| Method | Source |
|---|---|
| **Conclude inline** — the subplot's own narrator declares it resolved | Screenplay resolution, AW clock completion |
| **Converge** — the subplot merges into the main plot at its payoff moment | Truby (crossover), Wolfe via Kress, 06-10 episode §5.3 |
| **Supersede** — external events invalidate the premise | Rhapsode Weaver, AW (threat destroyed by fiction) |
| **Fade** — stop mentioning it; the world moves on | TV writers (dropped threads), Sly Flourish (thrown-away secrets) |
| **Epilogue** — tie up remaining loose ends after the climax | Perkins ("pull back the curtain"), Gilligan (series finale wrap-up) |

### 5.7 The gardener-architect spectrum — where Rhapsode should sit

The research reveals a clear spectrum. At one extreme: **pure architects** (Snyder, Bell) who prescribe exact page numbers for every beat. At the other: **pure gardeners** (King, Martin) who discover subplots through character behavior and prune in revision.

Working professionals sit in the middle:
- **Gilligan** knows the destination but not the route; he plants compelling images (the M60) and lets the constraint force creative solutions.
- **Martin** knows the endpoint and major beats but lets minor subplots emerge; this causes sprawl when pruning fails.
- **Mulligan** builds a "volcanic" world (tone + toys, not details) and develops specifics in response to player choices.

For Rhapsode, the **Gilligan position** is most tractable: scenario authors define the destination (end conditions) and a few compelling plants (seed nodes with Foreshadowed state). The system generates the route using character-driven causation. Unresolved plants create constructive pressure. Active pruning (MICE end conditions, maximum intervals, supersession) prevents the Martin failure mode.

### 5.8 Pre-plan the chain; improvise the delivery

A critical authoring principle that emerges unanimously:

> **"Don't set up a plant and then search for the payoff. The plant and payoff have to come together, then be separated by the narrative development."** — Industrial Scripts

> **"Secrets don't become real until the PCs discover them. They might change. They might disappear."** — Sly Flourish

> **"Good fiction always begins with story and progresses to theme; it almost never begins with theme and progresses to story."** — Stephen King

These are not contradictory. The **arc** (plant → chain → payoff) is designed as a unit. The **delivery** (when each beat surfaces, how the player encounters it) is improvised in response to actual play. The system should hold the authored chain and release beats according to the dramaturgical rules above — but the chain itself is not generated on-the-fly; it's planned.

## Part 6 — Implications for Rhapsode

### 6.1 What's wrong with the 06-10 proposal, through this lens

The 06-10 episode's "autonomous narrator" generates beats on-the-fly, with no pre-planned chain. This violates the plant/payoff design principle: if the narrator doesn't know where a thread is going, it can't plant setups for future payoffs, and its beats will be connected by "and then" rather than "but/therefore."

The "importance" metric (graph centrality) is a structural measure that doesn't capture dramatic need. A thread can be graph-central but dramatically inert (the war is linked to everything, but it's in a boring phase). A thread can be graph-peripheral but dramatically urgent (a single poisoned cup, barely connected to anything, is about to be drunk). Dramatic salience requires **precondition matching** (what's ripe to fire?) and **emotional oscillation** (what direction does the mood need?), not centrality.

### 6.2 What the research supports building

1. **Subplot chains as authored plans** — each thread is a Story Spine: inciting incident → 2— "because of that" beats → climax → resolution. These can be LLM-generated at authoring time (scenario design or narrator seeding), but they are **plans**, not open-ended generation. Each beat must pass Mamet's test (who wants what / what if they fail / why now).

2. **Beat selection by salience + oscillation** — the system picks which thread advances using: (a) which thread's next beat has its preconditions most specifically met (Valve/Ruskin, storylet salience), and (b) which beat type serves the emotional oscillation (Laws).

3. **Countdown clocks for ticking threats** — threads with time pressure advance mechanically when the player doesn't engage them (Apocalypse World). The clock is the "push" mechanism. Each clock step is a villain journal entry: objective/move/tell (Colville).

4. **Three discovery paths per subplot** — when introducing a thread, seed it through at least three independent routes in the WorldGraph (Alexander, Mulligan). Include at least one proactive route (the subplot comes to the player via a villain's "tell").

5. **Plant/reminder/payoff tracking** — the system knows which beats in each chain are setups, reminders, and payoffs (Rule of Threes). When the gap between setup and payoff grows long, inject a reminder beat. Plants and payoffs should be conceived together (Industrial Scripts, Gilligan).

6. **Minimum and maximum intervals** — don't fire two subplot beats back-to-back (minimum spacing). Don't let a subplot go silent for more than N turns (maximum interval, the RimWorld backstop).

7. **End subplots by convergence when possible** — the best ending is when the subplot merges into the main plot (Truby, Wolfe/Kress). The Weaver's existing convergence path is the natural mechanism. Respect FILO nesting order (Card/Kowal).

8. **Active pruning prevents sprawl** — the Martin failure mode (subplots growing "like weeds") must be prevented. Unused threads should be retired (Sly Flourish), not left to accumulate. MICE end conditions provide the test: has the question been answered? Has the character accepted a new role? Has order been restored?

9. **Every beat is intention + obstacle** — before the LLM generates a subplot beat, the prompt should enforce Sorkin's drive shaft. Not "trackers move through woods" but "trackers want to find the trail before the rain washes it away." This is the quality gate between dramatic beats and mere exposition.
