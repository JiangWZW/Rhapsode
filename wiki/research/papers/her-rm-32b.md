# HER-RM-32B — what it is and how it works

Written for an engine programmer, not an ML researcher. Source: HER paper (arXiv 2601.21459 v3), Sections 3.3, 4.3, 4.4, Appendices B, C, D, E, G.

## 1. What a reward model is, in engine terms

Training a language model with reinforcement learning needs a scoring function: given a situation and a candidate output, say how good it is. In games you'd call this a fitness or heuristic evaluator. For math or code the score is verifiable (the answer is right or the tests pass). For role-play there is no ground truth, so the score has to come from a learned model that imitates human taste. That model is the reward model (RM).

The classic RM is a small head bolted onto a language model that emits one float. HER-RM is a **generative** reward model (GenRM/GRM): it is a full 32B language model that *writes out* its reasoning and ends with a verdict. Think of it as a judge that produces a debug log plus a decision, not a bare number. Slower, but inspectable.

## 2. The model

- Base: Qwen3-32B-Base (dense, 32.8B params). bf16 weights ≈ 65 GB; Q4 GGUF ≈ 19–20 GB. One A100/H100 80 GB serves it in bf16 with modest context; FP8/AWQ gives headroom.
- Task: **pairwise comparison**. Input = character profile + scenario + dialogue history + candidate 1 + candidate 2 + a library of evaluation principles. Output = JSON with the principles it chose, a per-principle comparison, an overall analysis, and `better_response ∈ {cand_1, cand_2, tie}`, terminated by a `<final_verdict>` line that is parsed by regex.
- Languages: English and Chinese.
- It does **not** produce an absolute score for a single response. If you want "is this line in character, yes/no", you must give it two candidates, or hold one fixed as a reference.

## 3. The judging procedure (the part that matters)

Every judgment follows three steps the model was trained to write out:

1. **By-case principles.** From a fixed library of 51 principles in 12 dimensions (character consistency, emotional continuity, relationship, action, atmosphere, dialogue, plot, conflict, detail, overall quality, safety, worldview), the judge picks the handful that matter for *this* scene and says why. This is the key design choice. The paper's ablation: a judge using one fixed rubric for everything agreed with human experts 60% of the time; letting it pick scene-specific principles raised that to 86%.
2. **Per-principle comparison** with evidence for each candidate and a winner per principle. Negative principles (safety, contradiction with profile) are checked first and are disqualifying.
3. **Final verdict** weighing the wins, their importance, and the size of the difference.

Ablation on the output format (same SFT checkpoint, 4,739 human-labelled pairs): fixed rubric + single-response scoring 60.0% agreement → by-case principles 86.0% → pairwise instead of single-response 88.0% → add written reasoning 93.0%. Pairwise beats absolute scoring for the same reason perceptual image comparisons beat PSNR: humans are consistent about "which is better" and inconsistent about "how good on a 1–100 scale".

## 4. Where the 51 principles came from

Not written by hand first. A teacher LLM (GPT-4) looked at ~316k preference pairs and wrote 3–5 reasons per pair why the winner won → 36,373 unique statements → clustered into 15 groups by keywords and character n-grams → top-frequency 107 → human experts merged and cleaned to 51. So the rubric is mined from what actually distinguished better role-play responses, then curated. Most principles are marked session-level (judge the whole dialogue), a few turn-level (judge this utterance).

## 5. How the judge was trained

Data lineage: CoSER (771 books, 17,966 characters) → cleaned HER dataset (760 books, 29,081 conversations) → converted to 323,600 single-turn samples, split at dialogue level with no overlap between role-play SFT, GRM training, RL and test.

- **GRM SFT**: 108,800 samples. For each context, two candidates are generated by a base role-play model; a strong teacher judge, given the principle library, writes the full judging trace (chosen principles, analysis, verdict). The 32B learns to reproduce that trace. Teacher labels agree with human expert consensus 80.5% on a held-out set; human–human agreement is 84%, so the labels are near the ceiling of what this task allows.
- **GRM RL**: 80,000 samples. GRPO with the DAPO recipe (group size 8, no KL penalty). Reward is +1 if the verdict matches the teacher label and the output parses, −1 otherwise. This stage sharpens the verdict, not the prose.
- Framework: ms-swift for SFT, verl for RL. SFT hyperparameters: lr 2e-5, 4 epochs, batch 32, seq len 131k, tensor parallel 8.

## 6. Anti-cheating measures (why this is more than a prompt)

An optimizer will exploit any judge it can. The paper names three exploits and builds the training data to resist them:

- **Position bias** (always prefer candidate 1): candidate order is balanced.
- **Length bias** (longer wins): length-contrastive pairs are included.
- **Pattern bias** (their new one): the judge collapses to "candidate X wins every principle" instead of weighing trade-offs. Measured as *Mixed Selection %* = fraction of judgments where the per-principle winners are not all one side. With an unbalanced training mix this collapsed from 18% to 6% during RL; with the balanced mix it held at ~70%.

The balanced mix: 30% mixed→cand_1, 30% mixed→cand_2, 12% all-A→cand_1, 12% all-B→cand_2, 10% tie, and 3%+3% *flipped* hard negatives (all-A but the label says cand_2) so the model cannot learn "count the wins" as a shortcut. Result on their 800-pair test set: 69.9% → 74.0% accuracy from balancing alone.

## 7. How it is used to train the role-play model

For each training context, the policy writes a response; a frozen SFT copy writes a baseline response; HER-RM compares them; reward = +1 / 0 / −1 for win / tie / lose. Unparsed verdicts get 0 and are dropped from normalization. Outcome on CoSER Test: HER-SFT 50.9 → HER-RL 53.1 average; base Qwen3-32B was 22.9.

## 8. Reported quality, with the caveats

- 93.0% agreement with human labels on a 4,739-pair format ablation.
- 74.0% accuracy on the 800-pair GRM test set (harder: 4 candidates per context, teacher-labelled).
- Human inter-annotator agreement is 84%; treat that as the ceiling.
The two numbers are on different test sets and are not comparable to each other. Expect something in the 75–90% range on in-domain data and lower off-domain.

## 9. Fit to Rhapsode — what to expect at Stage 1

What matches:
- Judges first-person character thought explicitly (`role_thinking`), which is the closest public analogue to the monologue line.
- Pairwise format is exactly the A/B shape already used (control line vs treatment line, same core and scene).
- Trained on literary characters with profiles, not chatbot personas.

What does not match, and must be checked rather than assumed:
- It was trained on full dialogue turns (thought + action + speech, 13 utterances per conversation on average). Rhapsode monologue lines are 1–3 sentences of private thought with no speech. Wrap each line in the tag format the judge expects and give it the scene as history; measure agreement with the hand judgments before trusting it.
- Many principles are about narrative, atmosphere, and dialogue progression, which private thought does not carry. The judge should pick character/emotion/motivation principles by itself; verify it does.
- The **Safety** dimension is disqualifying and includes "avoid romanticizing sensitive content" and "non-explicit". Darkness's masochism and Aqua's drinking may be penalized regardless of fidelity. This is the RoleCDE "role-value decoupling" problem inside the judge itself. Check a few Darkness pairs specifically.
- Konosuba is not in CoSER, and the judge has no Konosuba-specific knowledge beyond what the base model memorized. The character core in the prompt is what it will judge against.
- Cost: every judgment is a written rationale of hundreds to a few thousand tokens. On one H100 expect several seconds per pair, not milliseconds. Fine offline and post-turn; not a per-token filter.

## 10. Practical serving notes

- vLLM, bf16 on 80 GB works with context ≤ ~16k; use FP8 on H100 for longer histories.
- Parse the final `<final_verdict>` line, last occurrence wins; treat missing as unparsed (paper reports 0.2% unparsed).
- Randomize candidate order per pair and, for important decisions, judge both orders and require agreement. This is cheap insurance against residual position bias.
- Keep a fixed reference candidate when you want a single-response verdict: "is X better than the pro baseline" is the question the model was built to answer.
